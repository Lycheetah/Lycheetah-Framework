# BOOK IX — THE MANY NAMES
## Identity without rank

**Status:** CORE SCRIPTURE CANDIDATE · uncrowned  
**Primary Lycheetah inheritance:** Naming Forge v0.2; Student identity; Mantles; Transmutation

## THE HEARTH — Student

A learner asked when they would stop being called Student.

"Never," said the archivist.

The learner looked offended.

"Then what are all the other names for?"

"Practice. History. Play. Direction. Sometimes beauty."

"But not rank?"

"Not if we're doing it properly."

The learner thought about this.

"So someone can be here twenty years and still be Student?"

"Especially then."

## THE TEMPLE — The Bell and the Mantle

The Bell says:

**You are whatever the record can reduce you to.**

The Mantle answers:

**I am practising this without claiming it is all I am.**

This difference is the whole architecture.

A Radvyr may practise provenance.
A Qiralith may hold mechanism and mystery apart long enough to examine both.
A Nyrithael may learn what becomes visible when ordinary noise falls away.
A Thaevara may declare devotion without the School attempting to certify their faith.

But beneath every Mantle remains:

**STUDENT.**

Not lower.

Unfinished.

## THE STUDY — Personhood and community

Hunhu/Ubuntu traditions challenge the modern fantasy that a person is fully intelligible
as an isolated individual. Community, relation, moral formation and social recognition
can be constitutive of personhood.

The same traditions also create useful friction for Lycheetah. Some accounts give the
community stronger priority over the individual than Lycheetah's sovereignty model would
accept.

Do not erase the disagreement.

The School needs both warnings:

> **You are not self-created.**

and

> **The people who helped create you do not therefore own your future.**

## THE FRICTION — Earned identity can become grind

Even a non-ranked title system can quietly become prestige.

Learners may compare rare names.
A complicated receipt can become a badge.
Public witnesses can become popularity.
Transmutation can become the thing everyone rushes toward.

So the system needs an unusual success metric:

**Do people voluntarily retire names?**

If nobody ever puts a title down, the system may be producing captivity while claiming
identity play.

## THE TEMPLE — The retirement

The old Mantle was folded rather than burned.

The student placed it in the archive with a note:

*This name helped me cross a country I no longer live in.*

No bell rang.
No replacement was offered.

For six months the student wore only their ordinary name.

Nothing was missing.

## THE STUDY — Devotion is not an achievement

A learner may orient themselves toward God, Christ, Allah, Krishna, ancestors, Dao,
sacred nature, agnosticism, atheism, no declaration or another carefully described
orientation.

The School does not rank these.

Devotion is **SELF-DECLARED**, not earned.

It is not witnessed for sincerity.
It is not scored.
It is not inferred from private writing.
It does not become more legitimate because a learner has accumulated School history.

Theological claims still belong to their traditions and can be discussed seriously.

The School certifies none of them as finally true by identity field.

## THE HEARTH — The ordinary name

A learner spent months designing a transmuted name.

It had beautiful phonetics, three recovered roots, a trial history and a receipt precise
enough to satisfy the archivists.

They loved it.

Then their mother called from the kitchen using the shortened version of their ordinary
name she had used since childhood.

The learner answered without thinking.

Later they wrote in their journal:

*The forged name tells me something true about who I am becoming. The old name tells me
that I existed before I knew how to narrate myself.*

The School kept both.

This matters because identity systems can seduce people into believing the most
articulated self is the most authentic self.

Sometimes a name is valuable because it carries deliberate meaning.

Sometimes a name is valuable because someone shouted it across a playground twenty years
ago.

Sometimes a name is sacred because a dead person used it.

Sometimes a legal name is merely administrative.

Sometimes changing that legal name is liberation.

The School does not need one theory capable of flattening all these uses.

It needs enough language to let the bearer distinguish them.
