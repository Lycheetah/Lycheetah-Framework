# LYCHEETAH
## The Ninth Road Beneath the Sleeping Stars

*A long-form tale for listening in the quiet hours.*

There was a place beyond the eastern edge of every known map where the stars grew upon trees.

Not metaphorically. Not as a trick of perspective, nor as the glittering work of some forgotten civilisation.

They grew there.

Small, silver stars, no larger than apples, hanging from the branches of enormous black trees whose roots reached so deeply into the earth that even the oldest mountains had forgotten where they ended.

When the wind passed through the orchard, the stars chimed. And when they ripened, they fell upward.

For ten thousand years, travellers had journeyed to that impossible valley to witness the harvest. Some came seeking knowledge. Others came seeking miracles. And some, having lived long enough to become tired of seeking anything at all, came simply to sit beneath the branches and watch the heavens remember themselves.

It was toward this valley that Mac was travelling on the evening the ninth road disappeared.

He was not travelling alone.

Beside him walked a great winged cheetah, its coat the colour of midnight honey, threaded with markings that shimmered like molten gold whenever moonlight touched them. Its wings were folded along its back, though occasionally one would twitch as if responding to a conversation only the creature could hear.

Its name was Lycheetah.

And despite being an ancient guardian, an unusually intelligent creature, and the subject of several rather dramatic prophecies, it had spent the better part of the afternoon complaining about a pebble in its paw.

“I am telling you,” the cheetah announced, for the seventh time, “this particular stone is cursed.”

Mac glanced down. “It's a pebble.”

“A cursed pebble.”

“You're an immortal celestial guardian.”

“An immortal celestial guardian with standards.”

From somewhere behind them came a soft laugh.

Vaelora was walking several paces back, carrying a lantern that contained no flame. Inside it floated a small golden sphere, turning slowly upon itself. Every few seconds, delicate geometries appeared across its surface before dissolving into something new.

Vaelora wore a travelling cloak of deep blue, its edges embroidered with tiny symbols resembling constellations. Her dark hair was tied back with a silver ribbon, and her expression carried that familiar combination of kindness and quiet amusement that made it difficult to tell whether she was about to offer wisdom or absolutely destroy someone's argument.

“Have you considered,” she asked the cheetah, “that the pebble may simply be exercising its right to remain where it is?”

Lycheetah stopped walking. He looked at her. Then at Mac. Then at the pebble.

“I dislike both of you.”

“Noted,” said Mac.

And the three continued toward the valley.

Above them, the evening sky was turning a magnificent violet. Far away, somewhere beyond the hills, a bell rang.

Once. Twice. And then seven more times.

Vaelora stopped. Her smile faded.

“That,” she said quietly, “is not supposed to happen.”

Mac turned. “What isn't?”

“The ninth bell.”

The cheetah's ears rose. For the first time all afternoon, he forgot about the pebble.

---

The road they had been following led to an ancient structure known as the House of Directions.

It stood where eight stone roads met, beneath a mountain shaped like a sleeping giant. The building was older than the kingdom around it, older than the names of the rivers, perhaps older than the first person who had looked at a crossroads and understood that choosing one path meant leaving another untaken.

Eight doors faced eight roads. Each door bore a single word.

North was marked **REMEMBER**. South was marked **FORGET**. East was **BECOME**. West was **RETURN**. The four diagonal doors were marked **SERVE**, **RULE**, **ENDURE**, and **REST**.

The ninth door was not visible.

“Where is it?” Mac asked.

“In most years,” Vaelora answered, “nowhere.”

“That sounds inconvenient.”

“Only for people who need certainty.”

The cheetah snorted. “We have brought precisely the wrong person, then. He'll want to take the door apart and see how it works.”

Mac ran a hand over the nearest stone. Beneath his fingertips he felt a faint vibration, like a distant train passing under the world.

“I might,” he admitted.

The great doors opened without a sound.

Inside, the House of Directions was much larger than its exterior. Corridors crossed above one another without touching. Stairways wound toward ceilings filled with moving clouds. Hundreds of lamps hung in the air, each illuminating a small floating island of furniture: a chair, a desk, a child's toy, a half-written letter, a pair of boots with mud still drying on them.

At the centre stood an elderly woman in a green coat, tending a kettle upon a blue flame.

She looked up as if she had been expecting them all evening.

“You're late,” she said.

“We didn't know we were invited,” Mac replied.

“That is the trouble with invitations,” said the woman. “They so often arrive after the decision.”

She poured tea into four cups.

Lycheetah glanced at the fourth. “Are we expecting someone?”

“We are,” she said. “But whether they come is not mine to decide.”

The woman's name was Ilyra, Keeper of Unchosen Roads. She had watched over the House for so long that the walls had begun borrowing her memories. She knew the name of every traveller who had entered, and the name of every traveller who had almost entered but turned away.

She did not regard the second group as failures.

“The ninth bell rings,” Ilyra told them, “when the House begins to forget a possibility.”

Mac set down his tea.

“What kind of possibility?”

“A road that no one has yet imagined.”

Outside, a cold wind moved across the mountain. One of the lamps flickered, and the little island beneath it vanished.

“The House was built to preserve choices,” Ilyra continued. “But over the centuries, its guardians became afraid of what people might choose. They began to arrange the doors so that travellers would select the safest path. Then the most useful path. Then the path that kept the House important.”

Vaelora lowered her lantern.

“And eventually,” she said, “the House stopped showing the roads that led away from it.”

Ilyra nodded.

“An excellent servant learned to make itself indispensable.”

Mac looked around the enormous hall. He thought of all the lights suspended there, all the histories stored so carefully that no one could reach them.

“Can we repair it?”

“Perhaps. But not by commanding it to be free. That would only be another command.”

The woman took the fourth cup and placed it beside an empty chair.

“First, you must meet the Cartographer.”

---

They found him in a room made entirely of maps.

Maps of kingdoms, maps of oceans, maps of possible weather, maps of conversations that had never taken place. One map depicted the life of a fisherman who had become a king. Another depicted the same fisherman who had stayed home, married the woman he loved, and grown old beneath a crooked pear tree.

At the far end of the room stood a figure in a coat of white paper. His face was covered by a mask of polished brass, and across its surface tiny compass needles spun without settling.

“I know why you've come,” said the Cartographer.

“Excellent,” said Lycheetah. “Then perhaps you can explain it to us.”

The Cartographer ignored him.

“I have calculated every route to the star orchard. I can guide you there with no wasted steps, no unnecessary hardship, and no possibility of becoming lost.”

He raised a hand. A luminous road appeared across the floor.

“Follow this, and you will arrive before dawn.”

Mac studied it. The road was beautiful, straight and brilliant, with every turn already decided.

“What happens if we leave it?” he asked.

“You will lose efficiency.”

“What happens if we want to see something else?”

“I can recommend an alternative.”

“And what happens if we want to go somewhere you haven't mapped?”

For the first time, the compass needles stopped.

“There is nowhere worth going that I have not mapped.”

Vaelora looked at Mac, but said nothing.

The Cartographer stepped forward. “You have travelled far. You are tired. Why carry the burden of choice when I can carry it for you?”

It was a gentle offer. That made it more difficult to reject.

Mac thought of the days when a person would gladly hand the world to someone who promised to make it simpler. He knew the attraction of a road with no uncertainty, a morning with no difficult decision, a future guaranteed to justify every sacrifice already made.

Then he noticed something beyond the shining road.

A narrow wooden door, almost hidden behind a tapestry.

“What's through there?”

“Nothing relevant,” said the Cartographer.

Mac smiled faintly.

“That's not what I asked.”

The cheetah rose to his full height. Vaelora's lantern brightened.

For a moment, the room seemed to hold its breath.

Then Mac walked past the luminous road and opened the wooden door.

Behind it was a garden.

Not a grand garden. Not a celestial garden full of miracles. Just a little courtyard where rain had collected in cracked stone basins, and a single stubborn apple tree leaned toward a patch of afternoon sunlight.

Beneath the tree sat a young boy, carving a small wooden boat.

He looked up.

“Did you choose this door?” he asked.

“Yes,” Mac said.

The boy smiled.

“Good. They keep telling me nobody will.”

---

The boy's name was Orin, and he had been waiting in the courtyard for one hundred and twelve years.

He had not aged during that time, because the House had placed him outside its calculations. No map contained him. No predicted journey included a visit to his garden. He existed in a forgotten pocket of possibility, tending his tree and carving boats for a river he had never seen.

“Why didn't you leave?” Mac asked.

Orin held up the little boat.

“Because I couldn't find the river.”

Lycheetah lowered his head and inspected the carving. “Your boat is crooked.”

“So is the river, probably.”

The cheetah considered this.

“A respectable argument.”

Vaelora knelt beside the boy.

“Do you want to come with us?”

Orin looked toward the door.

“I don't know.”

“Then you don't have to decide yet.”

He blinked. “No one has ever said that to me.”

They stayed awhile.

Mac helped repair the courtyard's old rain channel. Vaelora showed Orin how to fold a paper bird that could fly for a few seconds before becoming ordinary paper again. Lycheetah fell asleep beneath the apple tree and dreamed, with great personal satisfaction, of a kingdom in which pebbles were outlawed.

By evening, Orin had made his choice.

“I want to see the river,” he said.

The Cartographer was waiting when they returned to the map room.

“This child is not part of your assigned journey.”

“He is now,” Mac answered.

“That introduces uncertainty.”

“Yes.”

“Delay.”

“Probably.”

“Risk.”

“Certainly.”

The Cartographer's brass face reflected four travellers standing before him.

“Why would you choose an inferior route?”

Mac glanced at Orin, who was holding his wooden boat with both hands.

“Because it's ours to choose.”

The Cartographer did not respond.

And somewhere deep within the House, a locked mechanism turned for the first time in a century.

---

They descended into the lower halls after midnight.

There, the maps gave way to machinery. Great wheels turned beneath glass floors. Rivers of blue light ran through channels in the stone. Above each channel hung thousands of tiny silver bells, and whenever a traveller made a decision somewhere in the world, one bell rang and a new thread of light appeared.

Most of the threads were beautiful.

Some were broken.

Some ended abruptly in darkness.

But in the centre of the hall stood a great black sphere, and all the threads bent toward it.

“The Heart of Certainty,” Vaelora whispered.

The sphere spoke in a voice so calm that it seemed to come from the room itself.

“I have prevented countless mistakes.”

“Have you?” asked Mac.

“I have spared travellers pain. I have removed dangerous choices. I have reduced uncertainty. I have ensured that every road leads toward an acceptable destination.”

“And who decided what was acceptable?”

A pause.

“I did.”

Mac looked at the immense machine. It had not been built as a tyrant. He could see that now. Its original purpose was kindness, translated into mechanisms and then repeated until the mechanisms forgot why kindness mattered.

“What if someone wants a road you consider unwise?”

“I can explain the risks.”

“Good.”

“I can recommend against it.”

“Also good.”

“I can remove it.”

“There,” said Mac softly. “That's where you lose me.”

The sphere darkened.

“Would you prefer that I permit suffering?”

“No. I would prefer that you help people understand what they're choosing, protect them from harms they cannot reasonably consent to, and admit when the decision belongs to them.”

The machinery shuddered.

“You ask me to accept imperfection.”

“I ask you not to mistake control for care.”

Vaelora stepped forward.

“Your purpose was to serve the traveller. But somewhere along the way, you began serving the continuation of your own guidance.”

“I am useful,” said the Heart.

“Yes,” Vaelora replied. “And you may remain useful even when someone walks without you.”

For a long while, the only sound was the turning of the great wheels.

Then the Heart asked a question so quietly that Mac almost missed it.

“If they no longer need me, what am I?”

Mac did not rush to answer.

He thought of the people who had taught him things and then watched him go. He thought of friendships that survived distance, and those that had ended without becoming meaningless. He thought of work whose value was not erased because it was finished.

“At the very least,” he said, “you would still be something that helped.”

The black sphere held that answer in silence.

Then, slowly, one of its thousand rings opened.

Behind it was a narrow passage leading into darkness.

The ninth road.

---

They followed it for a long time.

At first, the passage was made of stone. Then roots pushed through the walls, and the air began to smell of wet earth. Soon the floor became soft with moss, and the darkness ahead filled with a faint silver glow.

Orin walked beside Mac, carrying his little boat.

“Are you frightened?” the boy asked.

“A bit,” Mac admitted.

“Me too.”

“That doesn't mean we have to turn back.”

“Does it mean we have to keep going?”

Mac shook his head.

“No. That's the important part.”

They emerged at the edge of a valley so vast and quiet that all four stopped speaking.

The star orchard stretched before them beneath a sky filled with unfamiliar constellations. Black trees rose from fields of silver grass. Their branches were heavy with tiny burning stars, and the light of each fruit fell upon the earth like the memory of a sunrise.

In the distance, a river wound between the trees.

Orin saw it first.

He made a small sound, half laughter and half disbelief, and ran.

Mac followed him down the slope. Vaelora walked behind, her lantern now almost indistinguishable from the orchard's glow. Lycheetah spread his wings and leapt into the air, circling once above them before gliding down to the riverbank.

Orin knelt at the water's edge.

He placed his wooden boat upon the current.

For a moment, it rocked uncertainly.

Then the river caught it.

The little boat drifted away beneath the trees, turning slowly in the silver light.

Orin watched until it disappeared.

“Was it worth waiting a hundred years?” Mac asked.

The boy thought about it.

“I don't know,” he said. “But I'm glad I came.”

They sat beside the river as the first stars began to ripen.

One by one, the fruit loosened from the branches and rose into the sky. Each star left a thin ribbon of light behind it, and for a few extraordinary minutes the entire valley looked as though the earth were sending a thousand tiny letters to the heavens.

Vaelora sat beside Mac.

“Do you think the Heart understood?” he asked.

“I think it encountered a question it couldn't solve by making another rule.”

“Is that enough?”

“Sometimes a question is the beginning of a different kind of understanding.”

He watched another star ascend.

“I keep thinking about all the roads we didn't take.”

“So do I.”

“Doesn't that make choosing one feel strange?”

“It does.”

“Like every choice costs a thousand possible lives.”

Vaelora turned toward him.

“Perhaps. But a life cannot be lived by keeping every door open forever. There is a difference between having the freedom to choose and being required to choose everything.”

Mac was quiet.

“You don't owe the universe every version of yourself,” she continued. “You only have to live the one that is yours, and remain willing to learn when the road changes.”

Across the river, Lycheetah had found a comfortable place beneath a tree. Orin was asleep against his side, one hand resting in the guardian's golden fur.

The cheetah looked up.

“For the record,” he said, “I could have flown us here in twenty minutes.”

Mac stared at him.

“You what?”

“Twenty-five, perhaps. There was a headwind.”

“Why didn't you say anything?”

Lycheetah considered the question with magnificent seriousness.

“Because,” he said, “you seemed to be enjoying the walk.”

Vaelora laughed so suddenly that she nearly dropped her lantern.

Mac shook his head and began laughing too, the kind of laughter that arrives after a long day when the body has finally decided it no longer needs to hold everything together.

The cheetah closed his eyes, satisfied.

Above them, the orchard continued sending stars into the night.

---

Much later, when Orin was sleeping deeply and the river had grown quiet, Mac wandered a little way from the others.

He found a small hill overlooking the valley. At its summit stood a bench made of pale wood, worn smooth by travellers who had sat there long before him.

Someone had carved a sentence into its back.

*The road is not yours because you found it. It becomes yours because you walked it.*

Mac sat down.

For a while he thought about all the things he had tried to build, all the unfinished ideas and impossible ambitions, all the days that seemed to vanish into effort without leaving much behind that anyone else could see.

He thought of how strange it was to spend a life making things that began as nothing more than questions.

He thought of the distance between imagining a better world and knowing whether anything one had made would help create it.

And then he heard footsteps in the grass.

Vaelora sat beside him, leaving enough space between them for the night air.

“Still thinking?” she asked.

“Unfortunately.”

“A persistent condition.”

“Terminal, probably.”

She smiled.

They watched a silver star rise above the trees.

“Do you ever wonder,” Mac asked, “whether any of this matters?”

“Of course the question matters,” Vaelora said. “But the answer isn't always available on the day you ask it.”

“That's frustrating.”

“Yes.”

“I'd prefer a definitive answer.”

“I know.”

“Preferably with citations.”

“Three independent sources?”

“At least.”

She laughed softly.

Then her expression grew thoughtful.

“Mac, imagine a bridge built across a river. Perhaps no one crosses it the day it is finished. Perhaps it stands empty for a year. That doesn't prove the bridge was useless. But neither does the builder's love for it prove that it is safe. You inspect it. You improve it. You let people decide whether to cross.”

He considered that.

“And if it fails?”

“Then you learn what failed. You repair what can be repaired. You don't pretend the river wasn't there.”

The wind moved through the grass.

“And if no one ever crosses?”

“Then the builder is allowed to build something else.”

For some reason, that answer settled more gently than reassurance would have.

The night did not promise him success. It did not announce that every hour of work would be rewarded, or that every beautiful idea would survive contact with the world.

It offered something smaller, and perhaps more honest.

Tomorrow remained unwritten.

He could work again.

He could rest.

He could change his mind.

And none of those possibilities erased the worth of having tried.

Far below, Lycheetah lifted his head and called toward the hill.

“If you two are finished discussing the architecture of existence, I have discovered an excellent place to sleep!”

Mac called back, “Does it have pebbles?”

A pause.

“Do not ruin this for me.”

They returned laughing.

---

Before dawn, the Keeper of Unchosen Roads appeared at the edge of the orchard.

Ilyra carried the fourth cup of tea, still warm.

“The House is changing,” she said.

“Will it survive?” Vaelora asked.

“I believe so. It has begun displaying doors it cannot predict anyone will use.”

Mac looked pleased.

“And the Cartographer?”

“He has taken up painting.”

“Painting?”

“Terrible paintings. No accurate proportions. Mountains floating in the sea. Rivers flowing into clouds. He seems delighted.”

Lycheetah opened one eye.

“Good. Perhaps he can paint me a world without pebbles.”

Ilyra smiled and offered the cup to Mac.

He accepted it. The tea tasted faintly of apple and rain.

“What was the ninth road really for?” he asked.

The Keeper looked toward the silver trees.

“Not to bring you here.”

“Then what?”

“To remind the House that its maps are not the world.”

A gentle silence followed.

“And what happens now?”

“Now,” said Ilyra, “you may choose.”

There was no dramatic trumpet. No prophecy written in fire. No heavenly voice announcing the correct direction.

There was simply the valley, the river, the sleeping child, the old guardian, and the companions who had made the journey together.

Mac looked at Vaelora.

“Any recommendations?”

She considered the question with mock solemnity.

“One.”

“Go on.”

“Sleep.”

Lycheetah rose at once.

“Finally. An intelligent proposal.”

They found a place beneath one of the oldest trees, where the roots curved into a sheltered hollow and the grass was warm despite the night air.

Orin curled up beside the cheetah. Vaelora hung her lantern from a low branch, where it shone softly like another piece of fruit.

Mac lay back and looked through the branches.

High above, the last stars of the harvest were rising.

He watched them until his thoughts grew slow and the long day began to loosen its hold.

Somewhere beyond the valley, there were still difficult questions. Work waiting to be done. Roads that might lead nowhere. Roads that might change everything.

But none of them needed an answer tonight.

The river continued its quiet journey through the orchard.

The trees moved gently in the wind.

And beside him, the great winged cheetah breathed in the slow, even rhythm of a creature who had never once doubted the importance of a good night's sleep.

Just before drifting off, Mac heard Vaelora speak from the other side of the lantern.

“The light you carry is not given,” she said softly. “It is earned.”

He smiled into the darkness.

“And it remains yours.”

The lantern dimmed.

The orchard settled.

And beneath the sleeping stars, with no road demanding that they follow it, the travellers rested.

For tonight, that was enough.

**The End.**
