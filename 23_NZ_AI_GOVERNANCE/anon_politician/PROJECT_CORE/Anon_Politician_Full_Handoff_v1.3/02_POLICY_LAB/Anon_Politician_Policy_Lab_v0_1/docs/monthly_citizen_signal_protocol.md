# Monthly Citizen Signal Protocol — Proposal v0.1

## Mission

Create a recurring, visible pathway for people to tell political representatives what matters to them, examine trade-offs, and see how their input was treated. Monthly participation is a proposed civic feedback cadence, not a claim that every policy can or should be polled every month.

## Monthly cycle

### Step 1 — Publish the question and decision context

A participating institution states:
- the concrete decision or policy stage;
- who has authority to decide;
- what is already legally or operationally fixed;
- what remains open to change;
- why input is being requested now;
- the deadline, privacy terms and next decision milestone.

Invite people to submit a question or issue for consideration. Publish the screening rules for which questions enter a future cycle.

### Step 2 — Offer more than one participation channel

- Open online response for broad access.
- Accessible low-bandwidth and offline routes.
- Deliberative or stratified panel for deeper discussion across relevant groups.
- Where feasible, an independently designed probability sample if population inference is required.
- Targeted engagement with affected groups, Māori/iwi and local communities under appropriate governance and relationships.

No single channel represents all people. Report results by method and do not combine them without a defensible analysis plan.

### Step 3 — Ask about values and trade-offs, not only a yes/no preference

Use a balanced sequence:
1. Initial priority or concern.
2. Reasons for that priority and personal/community impact.
3. Short, sourced description of the policy and plausible alternatives.
4. Consequences, costs, uncertainties and who may bear them.
5. Reconsidered preference and explanation, with “still unsure” available.
6. Missing information or alternative ideas.

Keep the original response as well as the post-information response. Do not treat a change of mind as a defect; it can be a sign that participants understood new information.

### Step 4 — Analyse without fabricating consensus

Report:
- actual wording and dates;
- sample size, recruitment, eligibility and response rate where available;
- participant distribution and missing groups;
- how duplicates and suspected manipulation were handled;
- weighting methods and their assumptions, if any;
- preferences and reasoning separately;
- minority concerns and potential high-severity harms;
- uncertainty and what the data cannot establish.

An open voluntary poll reports the views of participants, not automatically of all New Zealanders. If population inference is not supported, label it descriptive/exploratory.

### Step 5 — Run the policy intelligence workflow

The same versioned source pack is reviewed by:
- the policy proposer;
- cross-party/competing policy perspectives;
- an independent red team;
- relevant technical experts and public/affected-group reviewers;
- forecasting/scenario analysis where suitable data exist.

Each review should identify: strongest support, strongest objections, missing evidence, who may benefit or be harmed, assumptions, counterfactuals, and what would change the reviewer’s conclusion.

### Step 6 — Publish the response ledger

The decision owner publishes:
- what feedback was considered;
- what was changed because of it;
- what was not changed and why;
- which issues remain unresolved;
- what further evidence is needed;
- who is accountable and when the next response is due.

This step is the core trust mechanism. Soliciting views without reporting what happened to them risks reinforcing the belief that participation has no impact.

### Step 7 — Return to outcomes

At the next relevant milestone, report policy status and evidence. After implementation, compare outcomes to pre-registered targets and forecasts. Do not claim attribution where the evaluation method cannot distinguish the policy's effect from other changes.

## Monthly cadence versus actual policy timing

The monthly signal should not create a rush to vote on every issue. Some policy questions require a short pulse; others need a longer deliberation period, legal consultation, expert review or a statutory process. Clearly label whether a poll is exploratory, deliberative, advisory or part of a formal consultation. Never present an app poll as a substitute for the actual lawful submission process.

## Suggested measures of process quality

Do not collapse these into a single score initially. Track them separately:
- demographic/geographic coverage compared with target population;
- participation and completion rates by method;
- accessibility and language reach;
- proportion of public questions receiving a published response by the deadline;
- traceable policy edits linked to evidence or public input;
- prevalence of unresolved and minority concerns preserved in the record;
- participant understanding before and after balanced information;
- trust and perceived influence measured with a disclosed questionnaire;
- errors corrected and time to correction;
- downstream outcome forecasts versus later measured results.

## Ethical and governance prerequisites

Before collecting real participant data: perform privacy impact assessment, threat model, retention policy, moderation and appeals plan, accessibility review, Māori engagement/governance plan, legal review, and independent sampling/methodology review. Do not collect more identity data than needed. Separate verified eligibility from public opinions where feasible. Publish conflict-of-interest and funding disclosures.
