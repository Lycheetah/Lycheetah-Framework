# Source Baseline and Novelty Discipline

Anon Politician should not present broad wellbeing analysis, policy cost-benefit analysis, consultation, stakeholder mapping, scenario analysis or legislative submissions as if they were newly invented. New Zealand already has institutional practice in these areas.

The Treasury's Living Standards Framework explicitly asks analysts to consider individual and collective wellbeing, institutions and governance, and Aotearoa's wealth, including distribution, resilience, productivity and sustainability. The Treasury's CBAx tool supports cost-benefit analysis and transparency about assumptions and evidence. Parliament provides established channels for select-committee submissions.

The potentially distinctive Anon Politician hypothesis is the integration and continuity of several functions in one reviewable loop:

- recurring public signals connected to specific policy questions;
- explicit separation of population inference from opt-in participation;
- reciprocal cross-perspective challenge, with no party owning the final arbiter;
- a versioned claim/dependency memory that queues downstream review when evidence changes;
- a structured disposition trail showing which feedback was acted on, considered but unchanged, deferred, or rejected with reasons;
- later comparison between promised, forecast and observed outcomes;
- blind evaluation against simpler methods to test whether this integration adds value.

This is a research hypothesis, not a validated novelty or efficacy claim. The first comparator should include existing public-sector policy analysis, not an intentionally weak baseline. If existing methods already provide an element well, Anon Politician should reuse it, interoperate with it, or narrow its distinct contribution.

## Specific source-derived facts for this case

The MCERT development-levies page states that 56 submissions were received for the November 2025–February 2026 consultation from councils, developers, industry groups, Māori housing organisations and individuals. It reports councils strongly supported replacing development contributions, while developers' views were mixed; some supported national consistency and transparency, while others raised concerns about over-charging. It lists four strongest feedback themes: levy-area definitions, calculation detail, whether costs should be recovered beyond 10-year long-term plans, and transition from the old regime.

Those statements describe the consultation record, not national public opinion. The submission count is not a probability sample; do not use it to infer what New Zealanders generally think.
