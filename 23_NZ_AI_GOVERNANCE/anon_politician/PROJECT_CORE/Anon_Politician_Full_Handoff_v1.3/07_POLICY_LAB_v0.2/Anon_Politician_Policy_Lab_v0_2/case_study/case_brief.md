# Case Brief: Housing-Growth Infrastructure Funding and Development Levies

**Anon Politician pilot case v0.1**  
**Information cut-off:** 16 October 2026 (New Zealand time)  
**Purpose:** Test a policy-development workflow, not endorse or oppose a policy or party.

## 1. Why this case was selected

This is an active policy process connected to the founder's question: how can more people build a secure future in Aotearoa? It contains legitimate trade-offs, involves several public and private actors, and has a forthcoming formal stage where the design of a system can be scrutinised. It therefore provides a practical context for connecting public priorities, evidence, adversarial challenge, forecasts, and later outcomes.

This selection is an experiment-design choice, not a claim that this is the country's most important policy question or that any party's approach is superior.

## 2. What official sources establish

The Ministry for Cities, Environment, Regions and Transport's Going for Housing Growth page describes three pillars:

1. Freeing up land for development and planning changes.
2. Improving infrastructure funding and financing.
3. Incentives for communities and councils to support growth.

The programme's stated objective is to improve housing affordability by increasing the supply of developable land. The existence of a stated objective is not proof that the policy package will achieve it.

The published programme describes a proposed shift from development contributions to development levies. According to the Ministry's description, the intention is for councils and infrastructure providers to charge developers a proportionate share of capital expenditure needed to service growth over the long term. The page states that levies would be calculated from expected growth and aggregate growth costs in each levy area; separate levies would remain for different services; charges would have regulatory oversight; and councils could phase in higher charges.

As of the 16 October 2026 page update, the Ministry says the Local Government (Infrastructure Funding) Amendment Bill is being prepared for introduction in the first quarter of 2027. It anticipates further input on calculation methodologies and disclosure requirements, and a select-committee process once the Bill is introduced.

These are source-derived descriptions of the programme and stated next steps, not independent impact findings.

## 4. Neutral research question

**How should growth-related infrastructure costs be allocated and disclosed so decision-makers can assess impacts on housing delivery, affordability, councils, existing ratepayers, developers and future residents?**

Sub-questions:

- What costs are included, how are they calculated, and how is the method independently checked?
- How are costs attributed across services, locations, development types, and time periods?
- Under what conditions might levies affect project feasibility, the timing of development, dwelling prices/rents, or the mix and location of homes built?
- Which costs and benefits accrue to existing residents, new residents, developers, councils, infrastructure providers, central government and future residents?
- What incentives does the design create for councils and developers?
- How should the system deal with uncertain growth projections, infrastructure overshoot/undershoot, and changes in construction or financing costs?
- What information would allow the public to understand whether a levy is proportionate and how money was spent?
- What evidence would cause policymakers to revise the rules?

## 5. Hypotheses to test, not conclusions

The pilot must turn claims into falsifiable hypotheses and avoid assuming the direction of effects. Examples:

- H1: Greater predictability and transparency of infrastructure charges may influence the timing or feasibility of some development projects. The direction and size of the effect must be measured.
- H2: Who ultimately bears the economic cost of levies may differ from the entity legally charged. This incidence needs evidence, not assumption.
- H3: Different councils and infrastructure types may experience substantially different effects because growth forecasts, existing capacity, capital costs, and funding constraints differ.
- H4: People may support the objective of enabling more homes while disagreeing about who should pay for specific infrastructure. Public input should capture both priorities and trade-offs.
- H5: An AI-supported decision trail may improve traceability of how public submissions are considered, but this must be compared with normal analysis rather than presumed.

None of these hypotheses is treated as established in this case brief.

## 6. Scenario families for analysis

These are research counterfactuals, not a claim that each is currently an official option.

- **S0 — Baseline:** specify the actual legal and policy baseline at the date of analysis, including already enacted changes. Do not confuse a past baseline with the current one.
- **S1 — Published proposal:** model the development-levy design once its detailed formula and requirements are available.
- **S2 — Specified alternative allocation:** compare one alternative cost-allocation mechanism, clearly identifying who proposed it or labelling it as an analyst-created counterfactual.
- **S3 — Sensitivity/stress test:** vary build costs, interest rates, demand, uptake, infrastructure timing, project viability, and forecast error. Do not choose ranges merely to favour one scenario.

For every scenario, state the causal model, assumptions, source datasets, horizon, confidence or uncertainty range, and failure conditions. Avoid collapsing multiple objectives into one weighted score unless the value choices and weights are public and independently scrutinised; prefer side-by-side metrics and explicit trade-offs.

## 7. Affected parties to consider

As relevant to the evidence and policy scope:

- prospective homeowners and first-home buyers;
- renters and households facing housing insecurity;
- existing homeowners and ratepayers;
- developers of different sizes and project types;
- councils and local communities;
- infrastructure providers and central government;
- rural, urban, and fast-growing regions;
- Māori, iwi and hapū, including applicable Te Tiriti obligations and interests;
- disabled people, young people, lower-income households, migrants, and people who face barriers to formal participation;
- future residents and future generations.

This is a prompt for inclusive analysis, not a claim that every group has a single view or that the listed groups are exhaustive.

## 8. What the pilot must not do

- Assert that a levy will necessarily raise or lower house prices.
- Treat the government's desired outcomes as verified outcomes.
- Treat public poll volume as a representative measure without a valid method.
- Treat a party's model as the arbiter of its opponent's ideas.
- Convert an unresolved hypothesis or model output into a fact.
- Erase minority submissions or contradictory evidence because they are inconvenient.
- Give a single composite score the appearance of being value-neutral.
- publish raw submissions or participant information without checking parliamentary rules, consent, privacy and safety.

## 9. What would count as a useful output

A reviewable policy packet that shows: the proposal and its source; a statement of the problem; public signal methods and their limits; source-linked factual claims; forecasts and limitations; the strongest critiques; replies and amendments; unresolved conflicts; decision-owner rationale; pre-registered outcomes; and later results against forecast.

The first version succeeds technically if it can preserve traceability and make these gaps visible. Whether it improves policy quality is a separate empirical question.
