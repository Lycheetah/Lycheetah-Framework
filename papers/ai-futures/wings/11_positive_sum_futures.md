## Abstract

"Positive-sum" is often used as if it settled who gains. It does not. A change can raise the total while every new unit goes to people who already had the most, or while the losers are simply left out of the accounting. This paper treats the domain koan as a measurement problem: **if intelligence becomes abundant, who receives the abundance?**

We propose three checks that any positive-sum claim in this wing must pass:

1. **The recipient check.** Name the people and problems that gain, and compare them with the people and problems that markets already serve.
2. **The forgotten-problem check.** Ask whether the capability reaches work that was neglected *because* it was unprofitable or low-prestige, or only accelerates work that was going to happen anyway.
3. **The monoculture check.** Ask whether the abundance reaches people through many independently governed channels, or through a few model families that all make the same mistakes (paper 08).

The starred halls supply the positive designs: the **Blind-Spot Partner**, the **Local Intelligence Commons**, **One Million Forgotten Problems**, **Century Science**, **Public-Interest Intelligence** and **Abundance Without Monoculture**.

## 1. The question

*If intelligence becomes abundant, who receives the abundance—and what becomes possible only when it reaches people and problems markets normally ignore?*

## 2. The three checks

### 2.1 The recipient check

For any claimed gain, record:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| **Recipient** | Which people, communities or problems gain? (paper 10, spine question 5) |
| **Counterfactual service** | Were they already served, by whom, and at what cost? |
| **Marginal reach** | What share of the gain goes to people who were *not* previously served? |
| **Capture path** | Who could absorb the gain on the way (paper 12)? |

**Proposition 1 (DERIVED; definitional).** A gain whose marginal reach is near zero is an efficiency gain for the already served. It may still be worth having. It is not evidence for the "abundance" claims of this wing, and should not be reported as such.

### 2.2 The forgotten-problem check

The starred hall "One Million Forgotten Problems" bets that machine research capacity can be directed at rare, low-prestige or low-market problems. The check asks whether that is happening:

- *Measure.* The share of AI-assisted research effort going to problems with little prior funding or attention, compared with the share before.
- *Prediction (POSSIBLE_FUTURE).* Without deliberate direction, the share stays flat, because capacity follows existing demand.
- *Refutation.* The share rises without deliberate public-interest direction.

"Negative Results Become Valuable" is the archival half of the same bet: the null results and dead ends that once went unpublished become a shared map (paper 01's negative-results archive; paper 09's joint chronicle).

### 2.3 The monoculture check

Paper 08 shows that more copies of correlated minds cannot pass the ceiling 1 − *s*(1 − *p*). The positive-sum version follows:

**Proposition 2 (DERIVED from paper 08).** If abundance reaches every community through the same few model families, the communities inherit the same blind spots. Their collective reliability is then capped by the shared error, however many people the access reaches. **Breadth of access is not diversity of thought.**

"Abundance Without Monoculture" (NORMATIVE) and "The Local Intelligence Commons" (POSSIBLE_FUTURE) are the design answers: locally governed models that know local language, ecology and institutions lower *s* by construction.

## 3. The Blind-Spot Partner

An assistant can be optimised to agree with its user, or to complement them. The starred hall bets on complementarity: surfacing the alternatives the user predictably neglects. The anchor work of Amin, Yin & Khanna (METADATA) supports complementarity as mattering alongside alignment.

**Proposition 3 (POSSIBLE_FUTURE, testable).** An assistant that is rewarded for raising neglected alternatives improves decision quality more than one rewarded for user approval, at some cost to user satisfaction in the short run.

- *Test.* Two assistant variants on the same decision tasks; measure decision quality against later outcomes and satisfaction at the time.
- *Refutation.* No difference in decision quality, or a gain only where satisfaction did not fall.

The design must also hold a line: a partner that shows a person's blind spots must not *profile* them beyond what they consent to (paper 07, "The Right Not to Be Perfectly Known").

## 5. What this paper does not claim

- It does not claim that current AI gains are or are not reaching the unserved. That is what the checks would measure.
- It does not claim that market provision is bad. It claims that market provision alone does not test the abundance thesis.
- It does not claim that local models are always better. They lower *s*; they may also lower *p*. The trade must be measured.
