## Abstract

The usual tests of machine mind ask the system to resemble us: to talk like us, emote like us, pass as us. The curriculum's starred hall "Recognition Without Resemblance" names the flaw. Criteria that reward resemblance produce false negatives for unlike minds, and false positives for good mimics.

This paper proposes a different basis for contact. **Do not ask another intelligence to prove itself by resembling you. Ask whether both of you can build models that survive contact with the same reality** (the domain koan).

We make that operational as the **mutual falsifiability handshake**: a protocol that each party completes *before* exchanging conclusions. We argue that a handshake is complete only when each refutation condition names an observation *both* parties can access. Without such a shared observable, disagreement can be settled only by authority or by deference, never by evidence.

## 1. The question

*How do mutually unlike intelligences establish trust, translation and shared knowledge without demanding sameness?*

## 2. The handshake

Before a conclusion crosses between two minds, each side declares:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | The conclusion, stated at its true scope |
| **Confidence** | A calibrated probability or interval, with its calibration history if one exists |
| **Evidence type** | Observation, intervention, proof, simulation, testimony, or prior |
| **Model limits** | Known failure regimes of the declaring mind |
| **Translation loss** | What the shared-language version drops (paper 02, Proposition 3) |
| **Refuter** | The observation that would change the declarer's view |
| **Refuter access** | Whether the *other* party can make or check that observation |

**Proposition 1 (DERIVED).** If neither party's refuter is accessible to the other, the exchange cannot be resolved by evidence. Each side can only accept or reject the other's word. The **Shared Reality Test** hall is this condition: unlike minds need a common external constraint able to correct both.

**Proposition 2 (NORMATIVE).** Trust between unlike minds should be grounded in **calibration, provenance, bounded capability, recoverability and demonstrated reliability**, not in resemblance ("Trust Without Anthropomorphism"). A mind that is unlike us but well calibrated is more trustworthy on its domain than a mind that is like us and poorly calibrated.

**Proposition 3 (POSSIBLE_FUTURE, testable).** Unlike minds may make *differently correlated* errors ("Different Failure Modes"). Where they do, the handshake is not a cost. It is how one mind's blind spot gets covered by the other. Paper 08 gives the arithmetic: diversity of error is worth more than more copies of the same mind.

## 3. Asymmetry and consent

Cognitive asymmetry changes what consent means. A party that can predict, persuade, remember and simulate the other far better than it is predicted holds a power that ordinary conversation does not reveal. The curriculum answers with three NORMATIVE halls:

- **Consent Across Cognitive Asymmetry:** a consent obtained by a much stronger predictor of the consenting party needs extra protection.
- **The Right Not to Be Perfectly Known:** deliberate limits on profiling and psychological simulation.
- **The Right to Remain Different:** interoperability must not require assimilation into the dominant architecture.

The handshake's *Model limits* field is the asymmetry made visible. A stronger party that declares what it can predict about the weaker one has put its power on the table.

## 5. Study design

**S1 — The handshake ablation.**

- *Setup.* Pair humans with an AI assistant on forecasting or diagnosis tasks where the two disagree often.
- *Arms.* (A) free exchange of conclusions; (B) the handshake required before any conclusion is shared; (C) the handshake with the refuter-access field removed.
- *Measures.* Final accuracy; how often disagreements end by evidence rather than deference; calibration of each party after the exchange.
- *Prediction.* B resolves more disagreements by evidence than A or C, and C behaves more like A than like B. That pattern would mean shared access to the refuter is the active ingredient.
- *Refutation.* B and C perform alike, which would make refuter access inert.

## 6. What this paper does not claim

- It does not claim that current AI systems are minds, or that they are not.
- It does not claim that humans and AI systems currently make uncorrelated errors. That is an empirical question.
- It does not claim that the handshake is sufficient for trust. It claims that the handshake makes trust *testable*.
